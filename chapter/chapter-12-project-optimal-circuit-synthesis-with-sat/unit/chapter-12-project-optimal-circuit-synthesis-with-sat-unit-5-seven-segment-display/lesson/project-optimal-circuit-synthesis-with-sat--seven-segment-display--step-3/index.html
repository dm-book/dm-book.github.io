Project: Optimal Circuit Synthesis with SAT · Seven-Segment Display

Lesson 3

Nikolai Chukhin · Alexander S. Kulikov

A straightforward construction computes an indicator for each decimal digit and ORs the required indicators for every segment. A SAT solver can often do better by discovering intermediate functions shared in less obvious ways.

The same method applies to instruction decoders, cryptographic S-boxes, arithmetic blocks, and error-correcting-code logic. One can also optimize depth instead of gate count, restrict the gate library to match a fabrication technology, or synthesize several outputs under a joint area bound. In every case the pattern is the same: encode candidate implementations, ask SAT for a construction, and use UNSAT to certify a lower bound.

For the curious 🤓
The first concerted attempt to optimize every function of four variables was published by Howard Aiken's staff at the Harvard Computation Laboratory in 1951. They worked by hand, using heuristic methods to construct extensive tables of the best circuits they could find; the tables did not prove that those circuits were optimal.Their circuits modeled vacuum tubes, so their cost \(V(f)\) was quite different from our number of two-input gates. They allowed NOR, OR, and NAND gates with \(k\) inputs at cost \(k\). An input to a gate could be a variable, a complemented variable, or the output of an earlier gate. At the top level they could take an AND of any number of gate outputs for free.This last convention made complementation asymmetric. For example, \(x\land y=\operatorname{NOR}(\bar x,\bar y)\) had cost \(2\), while \(\bar x\lor(\bar y\land\bar z)=\operatorname{NAND}(x,\operatorname{OR}(y,z))\) had cost \(4\). Its complement \(x\land(y\lor z)=\operatorname{AND}(\operatorname{NOR}(\bar x),\operatorname{OR}(\bar y,\bar z))\) cost only \(3\), because the final AND was free. Consequently the Harvard group had to consider \(402\) essentially different classes of four-variable functions instead of the usual \(222\) classes.Let \(S_{I}\) be the symmetric function that is one precisely when the number of ones among its arguments belongs to \(I\). The Harvard tables gave \(V(f)<20\) for every class except for \(64\) functions equivalent to \[S_{\{0,1\}}(w,\bar x,y,z)\lor\bigl(S_{\{2\}}(w,\bar x,y)\land z\bigr).\] They implemented these functions with \(20\) control grids. One of their constructions was \[\begin{aligned}g_1&=\operatorname{NOR}(\bar w,\bar x), & g_2&=\operatorname{NAND}(\bar y,z),\\ g_3&=\operatorname{NOR}(w,\bar x),\\ f&=\operatorname{AND}\bigl(\operatorname{NAND}(g_1,g_2),\\ &\qquad \operatorname{NAND}(g_3,\operatorname{NOR}(\bar y,\bar z)),\\ &\qquad \operatorname{NOR}(\operatorname{NOR}(g_3,\bar y,z),\operatorname{NOR}(g_1,g_2,g_3))\bigr).\end{aligned}\]The first computer program to find provably optimal circuits was written by Leo Hellerman in 1963. It found the minimum number of NOR gates for every function of three variables. Gate inputs had to be uncomplemented variables or earlier gate outputs, and both fan-in and fan-out were at most \(3\). Among circuits with the same number of gates, Hellerman minimized the total number of gate inputs. Thus, for example, \(\bar x=\operatorname{NOR}(x)\) used one gate, while \(x\lor y\lor z=\operatorname{NOR}(\operatorname{NOR}(x,y,z))\) used two.Knuth later carried out the complete computation for five variables in the unrestricted two-input gate model used in this project. Instead of enumerating all \(2^{31}\) normal truth tables, he worked with the \(616{,}126\) equivalence classes obtained by permuting and complementing inputs and complementing the output. Generating all members of a class from one representative gave approximately a thousand-fold speedup. He also combined bottom-up and top-down searches and transferred bounds between related functions. For example, from a circuit of size \(r-2\) for \(f\) one immediately obtains a circuit of size at most \(r\) for \[f(x_{1}\land x_{2},x_{1}\lor x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5}).\]After finding every class of cost at most \(10\), these methods produced circuits of size at most \(11\) for all but seven classes. Knuth then generated approximately \(53\) million special wiring patterns of length \(11\). Assigning one of the five nontrivial normal operations to each gate made every pattern yield \(5^{11}=48{,}828{,}125\) resulting functions. Six of the seven remaining classes had an \(11\)-gate circuit. The sole survivor, represented by the hexadecimal truth table 169AE443, is the unique class with circuit complexity \(12\). Its minimum formula length is also \(12\), so sharing intermediate values does not help for that representative.Modern exact synthesis usually asks the same fixed-size existence question with a SAT solver. Work on SAT encodings, topology families, and parallel solving has made this a practical component of logic-optimization systems. Recent tools can also optimize a small part of a large circuit while exploiting don't-care cases introduced by the surrounding network; for example, eSLIM (2024) uses SAT-based exact synthesis for such local replacements. This proves the replacement optimal under its local specification, not necessarily the entire network optimal. The historical account and Knuth's five-variable computation are from The Art of Computer Programming, Vol. 4A, Section 7.1.2.
