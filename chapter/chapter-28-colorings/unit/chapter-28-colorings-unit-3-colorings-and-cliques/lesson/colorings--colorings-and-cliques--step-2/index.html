Colorings · Colorings and Cliques

Lesson 2

Nikolai Chukhin · Alexander S. Kulikov

We already know that the chromatic number of a graph cannot be less than its clique number: \[\chi(G) \ge \omega(G).\] It is natural to ask: is it true that if the chromatic number is high, then there is also a large clique? In other words, is a large number of colors always caused by the presence of a large clique? It turns out that in general this is not the case: there exist graphs with arbitrarily high chromatic number that have no nontrivial cliques. Moreover, there are graphs in which the neighborhood of every vertex is a tree (and thus can be colored with two colors), yet the graph itself has a high chromatic number. For this reason, the chromatic number of a graph is a nonlocal property (unlike, for example, its degree): you cannot look at the neighborhood of each vertex and determine what the chromatic number will be. On the other hand, there are various classes of graphs for which the equality \(\chi(G)=\omega(G)\) holds.

For the curious 🤓
A graph is called perfect if for every induced subgraph \(H\) we have \(\chi(H)=\omega(H)\). This is a surprisingly large class: bipartite graphs, chordal graphs, and interval graphs are all perfect, and the complement of a perfect graph is again perfect.One of the central results here is the Strong Perfect Graph Theorem: a graph is perfect exactly when it contains neither an induced odd cycle of length at least five nor the complement of such a cycle. Perfect graphs are also algorithmically pleasant: despite graph coloring being hard in general, the chromatic number of a perfect graph can be found in polynomial time!
