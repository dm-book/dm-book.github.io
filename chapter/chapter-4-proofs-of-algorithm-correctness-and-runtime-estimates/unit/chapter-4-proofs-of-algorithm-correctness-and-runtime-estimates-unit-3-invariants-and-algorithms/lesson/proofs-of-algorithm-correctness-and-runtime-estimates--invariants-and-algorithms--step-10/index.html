Let's show that nine moves are sufficient for any sequence. Indeed, in the first move, you can put the volume \(0\) in its place; in the second move, you put volume \(1\) in its place. Thus, after nine moves, the volumes \(0, \dotsc, 8\) will be in their places, which means the volume \(9\) will also be in its place.

Continuing our example above, we first exchange the volumes \(0\) and \(6\) (at positions \(7\) and \(0\), respectively). Here is an example.

Then, we exchange the volumes \(1\) and \(9\), at positions \(9\) and \(1\).

At this point, the volume \(2\) occupies its position, so exchange volumes \(3\) and \(7\), at positions \(4\) and \(3\).

Thus, after the first three moves, the first four volumes are in their right positions. Continuing in the same spirit, we maintain the following invariant: the first \(i\) volumes can be put in their places after no more than \(i\) moves. Thus, nine moves will be sufficient for any sequence.

Problem. Have we proven that the minimum number of moves required to sort any sequence of ten volumes is nine?
