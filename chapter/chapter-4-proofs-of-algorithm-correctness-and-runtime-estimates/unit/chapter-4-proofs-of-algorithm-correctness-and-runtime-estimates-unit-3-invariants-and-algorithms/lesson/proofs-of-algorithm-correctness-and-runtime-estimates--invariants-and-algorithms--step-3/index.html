Lesson 3

Nikolai Chukhin · Alexander S. Kulikov

Let's try to fill the first two rows somehow so that their sum equals twenty.

Since the sum in each column must equal ten, the third row is determined uniquely.

However, the sum of the numbers in the third row turns out to be ten. Thus, our first attempt did not work, but this does not mean that the mission is impossible: it is still not excluded that if we had started with other numbers in the first two rows, everything would have matched.

If you look closely at the table above, it becomes clear that the sum in the third row turned out to be ten not by chance: since the sum in each of the five columns equals ten, the sum of the numbers in the entire table equals fifty; and since the sum in each of the first two rows equals twenty, the sum in the third row equals \(50-20-20=10\). Therefore, it is not possible to fill the table as required.

The invariant in this problem is the sum of the numbers in the entire table. There is no process in this problem: we simply understand that the sum of the numbers in the entire table should not change depending on whether we sum by rows or by columns. Therefore, recognizing this invariant in the current problem is not difficult. Next, we will see problems where guessing the necessary invariant will be more challenging.

The technique we used to solve this problem is called double counting.
